# Lesson 3 — The Plasma Membrane and Transport

## Structure of the membrane

The plasma membrane is made mostly of phospholipids. A phospholipid has a head that contains a phosphate group and two long tails made of fatty acids. The head is hydrophilic, meaning it is attracted to water. The tails are hydrophobic, meaning they avoid water.

In water, phospholipids arrange themselves into a phospholipid bilayer: two layers with the heads facing outward toward the watery fluid on each side and the tails pointing inward, away from the water.

Scientists describe the membrane with the fluid mosaic model. It is "fluid" because the phospholipids can slide sideways past one another. It is a "mosaic" because many different proteins are embedded in the bilayer, like tiles in a mosaic. In animal cells, cholesterol molecules sit between the phospholipids and keep the membrane from becoming too stiff or too runny.

## Selective permeability

The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances cross and blocks others. Small molecules that are not charged, such as oxygen (O₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂), slip directly through the bilayer. Ions, such as sodium ions, and large polar molecules, such as glucose, cannot pass through the tails of the bilayer; they need help from membrane proteins.

## Passive transport

Passive transport is the movement of substances across the membrane without the cell using any energy. Substances move down their concentration gradient, from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.

**Diffusion.** Particles are always moving randomly. Because of this random motion, there is a net movement of particles from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated, until they are spread evenly. This even state is called equilibrium; particles still move, but there is no longer a net movement in one direction. Diffusion is faster at higher temperatures and when the difference in concentration is larger.

**Facilitated diffusion.** Some substances diffuse across the membrane through transport proteins. Channel proteins form tunnels that particular ions can pass through. Carrier proteins bind a specific molecule, such as glucose, change shape and release it on the other side. Facilitated diffusion is still passive: no energy is used, and substances still move from higher to lower concentration. Water crosses many membranes quickly through channel proteins called aquaporins.

## Osmosis

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves from the side with the lower solute concentration to the side with the higher solute concentration. (The side with less dissolved solute has more free water, so water moves toward the side with more solute.)

We compare the solution outside a cell to the inside of the cell:

- In a **hypotonic** solution, the outside has a lower solute concentration than the cell. Water moves into the cell. An animal cell swells and may burst, which is called lysis. A plant cell swells until its membrane presses against the cell wall; the wall keeps it from bursting, and the cell becomes firm, or turgid. This is the healthy state for most plant cells.
- In a **hypertonic** solution, the outside has a higher solute concentration than the cell. Water moves out of the cell. An animal cell shrivels. In a plant cell, the membrane pulls away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis, and the plant wilts.
- In an **isotonic** solution, the solute concentration is the same inside and outside. Water moves in and out at equal rates, so there is no net change in the cell's size. This is the healthy state for animal cells, which is why medical IV fluids are made isotonic to blood.

## Active transport

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient, from lower concentration to higher concentration. Because this is "uphill," it requires energy, usually supplied by ATP. Active transport is done by protein pumps in the membrane.

The best-known example is the sodium-potassium pump. Each time it uses one ATP, the pump moves three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into the cell. Nerve cells depend on this pump to send signals.

## Moving large particles

Some particles are too large to pass through any protein. Cells move them in membrane bubbles called vesicles, which also requires energy.

- **Endocytosis** brings material into the cell: the membrane folds around the material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell. When a cell engulfs a large particle such as a bacterium, this is called phagocytosis, or "cell eating." White blood cells use phagocytosis to destroy bacteria.
- **Exocytosis** sends material out of the cell: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside. Cells release hormones and other proteins this way.
