# Lesson 1 — Cell Theory and Types of Cells

## Discovering cells

In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork through a simple microscope. He saw tiny empty boxes and called them "cells" because they reminded him of the small rooms monks lived in. What Hooke saw were the walls of dead plant cells.

A few years later, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek built better lenses and became the first person to observe living single-celled organisms, which he called "animalcules," in pond water.

In 1838 Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells, and in 1839 Theodor Schwann concluded the same for animals. In 1855 Rudolf Virchow argued that new cells are produced only by the division of existing cells.

## The cell theory

Together, these discoveries became the cell theory, which has three parts:

1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.
3. All cells come from pre-existing cells.

A living thing made of just one cell is called unicellular. A living thing made of many cells is called multicellular.

## Why cells are small

Most cells are between 1 and 100 micrometers across, far too small to see without a microscope. Cells stay small because of the relationship between surface area and volume. Everything a cell takes in or gets rid of must cross its outer surface, the plasma membrane. As a cell grows, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so a large cell would not have enough surface to exchange materials quickly enough for all of its volume.

Example: a cube-shaped cell 1 micrometer on each side has a surface area of 6 square micrometers and a volume of 1 cubic micrometer, a surface-area-to-volume ratio of 6 to 1. A cube 2 micrometers on each side has a surface area of 24 square micrometers and a volume of 8 cubic micrometers, a ratio of only 3 to 1. The bigger cell has less surface for each unit of volume.

## Features all cells share

Every cell has four things in common:

- a plasma membrane, the boundary that separates the inside of the cell from its surroundings;
- cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid and everything suspended in it inside the membrane;
- ribosomes, the small structures that make proteins;
- DNA, the genetic material that carries the instructions for the cell.

## Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

Cells are divided into two basic types.

**Prokaryotic cells** do not have a nucleus. Their DNA sits in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Prokaryotic cells also have no membrane-bound organelles. They are usually small, about 1 to 5 micrometers. Most prokaryotes have a cell wall outside the plasma membrane, and many have a flagellum, a whip-like tail used for movement. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes. Every prokaryote is unicellular.

**Eukaryotic cells** have a nucleus, a compartment surrounded by a membrane that holds the cell's DNA. They also contain many membrane-bound organelles, small structures that each do a specific job. Eukaryotic cells are usually much larger, about 10 to 100 micrometers. Animals, plants, fungi and protists are made of eukaryotic cells.

A quick way to remember: "pro" sounds like "no" — prokaryotes have no nucleus. "Eu" sounds like "do" — eukaryotes do have a nucleus.

## Looking at cells

A light microscope uses visible light and glass lenses and can magnify up to about 1,000 times. It can be used to watch living cells. An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light and can magnify much more, enough to see the inside of organelles, but the specimens must be specially prepared and are no longer alive.
